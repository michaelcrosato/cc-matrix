3. Specify the repair and deduction rules as real game systems.

Represent each repair as an explicit state machine. A targeted component starts attached at both terminals. Cut each terminal, grip and remove the freed part, place the required jumper or replacement in its socket, and solder both terminals. A completed component visibly becomes stable and tested. Complete the ordered list of required components to secure the board. Each input gives an immediate legal-target highlight, tool response, and concise result; finishing an animation alone never counts as a repair.

Work orders identify components with position, silhouette, and an ID or pattern. Repeat the current order on demand at no penalty. Retain the latest meaningful instruction on screen until it is satisfied. A short order strip can show the next two tasks; opening the full order uses the same readable shell as other menus. Teach the full sequence with a friendly guided repair, then fade assistance as the player demonstrates it.

Replacements obey one rule per board: matching shape or matching color identity. Give the player a consistent way to discover it through a test socket and example markings. Testing a spare consumes a small, visible amount of fuse time and reports continuity; it does not destroy stock. Players can test safely, infer from prior examples, or take a faster risk. A board's rule never changes after discovery. Ensure each offered correct replacement is unambiguous under that rule. Express color identity with a redundant pattern and label so every rule remains playable without color discrimination.

The tray includes enough spares and jumpers to complete every legal repair. A recoverable error must leave a repair route and a usable replacement. If a part is dropped, return it to a clear tray or salvage location. If a player leaves a board, persist the board exactly, including incomplete joints and heat. Returning to evidence never repairs damage or clears a hazard for free.

Design three understandable error paths. Cutting an incorrect terminal or violating the work order starts a clearly marked recovery window: restore the highlighted joint before a time/heat penalty applies. Installing a wrong replacement produces a failed continuity check and requires removal and replacement. Overworking the soldering iron raises visible heat; a fire requires the extinguisher before the board can be secured. Show what happened, the recovery action, and the time consequence. Use forgiving starting values—about an eight-second recovery window and brief solder holds—then expose and tune them. Avoid invisible instant losses during ordinary tool work.

Soldering is a controlled hold with a visible safe completion band. Releasing in the band finishes the joint; excess holding adds heat. Focus snapping and a precision modifier help controller users. Touch assistance can finish a legal joint at its safe threshold. Extinguishing uses an obvious target and brief hold; its feedback must remain readable through foam and sparks. Cooling continues according to simulation time even when a board is not being viewed. An established fire stays active until extinguished or the case ends; cooling alone cannot erase it. Board selection and the persistent HUD show active fire and recovery deadlines, including on boards the player has left.

For deduction, use **one 4-by-5 binary stencil per digit**. Unknown cells, confirmed illuminated cells, and confirmed dark cells have distinct visual treatments. Dark evidence is just as meaningful as lit evidence. A successful repair reveals a small authored pack of cells in one digit rather than demanding a full circuit for every single cell. This preserves the source's two-scale relationship while supporting a tightly paced demo.

Define ten distinct digit glyphs and compute candidate compatibility against their actual cell data. Provide an always-accessible reference using those exact glyphs: the code-entry dial previews its selected digit in stencil form, and readable help lets players inspect all ten. The guaranteed evidence route must reduce every digit to exactly one candidate. An optional earlier route may do so with fewer boards. Permit risky code entry after two successful boards; the one-board guided practice explicitly bypasses this gate to teach confirmation. Clearly distinguish the act of selecting digits from the deliberate final submission. Additional boards offer certainty and time/score tradeoffs. Never require a blind guess to finish a case. Keep automatic candidate elimination as an optional assistance setting; the default view lets the player read the stencil and reason.

Create a solver and content validator that use the same glyphs and masks as the game. Verify useful evidence, eventual unique identification, reachable sockets, valid order references, recoverable errors, sufficient stock, and an exit from every nonterminal repair state. Reject authored or generated content that fails. Seeded variation changes codes, spare arrangements, and compatible board templates while preserving these invariants. Choose the code and all clue truth once at case start.

Score successful disposal, remaining fuse time, clean work, and informed early submission. Explain the score with a few large result cards. Use score as a reason to replay; assistance and developer-modified runs are labeled in their results. Preserve a seed for retrying the same case and offer a separate fresh-seed replay. A wrong final code and an expired timer each resolve exactly once, with the cause stated plainly.
