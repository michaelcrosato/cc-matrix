# Research and source interpretation

Inspected on 2026-11-06. The source is **Space Fortress, Century Electronics / CVS, 1981**. It is not the Astrocade four-direction game, the Zaccaria Star Castle derivative, or the cognitive-training benchmark. Some catalogue aggregations mix those works; this project follows the Century release's three defensive situations.

## Historical evidence actually inspected

- **Century UK sales flyer**, [archive record](https://flyers.arcade-museum.com/videogames/show/3233) and [original illustration and text](https://flyers.arcade-museum.com/videogame-flyers/3/space-fortress-03229-01.jpg). Downloaded as `source-flyer.jpg` and inspected visually. It depicts a large illuminated station with multiple mechanical sections in a painted space setting. The brief identifies fortress 13, the Viper interceptor, Dronian attackers, hunters, scouts, drones, gravity bombs, energy accumulation, radiators, and promotion. The copy's exaggerated military urgency informed the game's short captions. No flyer artwork is used as an in-game asset.
- **Museum of the Game**, [Century Space Fortress entry](https://www.arcade-museum.com/Videogame/space-fortress). Read the description, introduction, specifications and technical notes. These support the Asteroids-like free-flight stage, center-station defense, bottom-of-screen horizontal last stage, and tractor hazards. The museum reports color raster graphics and early speech. Its control specifications and prose are not completely consistent, so this game does not claim to reconstruct an original control panel.
- **MobyGames**, [game 233640](https://www.mobygames.com/game/233640/space-fortress/). Direct open returned HTTP 403; the search tool returned the full cached description, which was read. It describes loss-driven stage progression, gravity-bomb pull, fuel replenishment from destroying a bomb, and ground-base damage/repair. These remain secondary-source claims. The new game uses boost charge and cooling rather than removing ordinary thrust when fuel is empty.
- **Representative gameplay video**, [hirudov: Arcade Longplay — Space Fortress (1981) Century Electronics](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z43SQdcM7Nk), 9m14s. Downloaded the video's 640×360 picture stream for research, then inspected a 12-frame contact sheet at 45-second intervals in `footage-contact.jpg`. Frames show the sparse starfield, small bright multicolored interceptor and attackers, central green mechanical fortress, later green/purple ground installation with a small gun along its top, and a military rank/score registration screen. This is visual inspection of sampled footage, not a full real-time viewing or an audio listening claim. The older [2019 longplay](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2DvSKx76r50) was unavailable during inspection. The [Old Classic Retro Gaming recording](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gcsiwDpQ0cI) was found as another specifically identified Century recording; its description was read, but its footage was not inspected.
- **MAME primary implementation**, [CVS driver](https://github.com/mamedev/mame/blob/master/src/mame/misc/cvs.cpp), read via raw master source. It corroborates Century CVS, starfield/color-raster implementation, TMS speech support and the 1981 Century `spacefrt` entry. The moving master revision was not resolved; exact MAME commit is **unknown**. The source itself notes approximate discrete sound emulation, so it is not evidence for exact original sound design.

## Deliberate departures

The new title is **Space Fort After Dark**, selected with a real random draw from 50 candidates; `title-draw.json` records the draw. It is an original homage, not an emulation. The three scenarios become a finite siege with success-driven progression as well as forced retreats, a winnable recurring siege-ship boss, simultaneous one-to-four-player cooperation, selectable run upgrades, readable telegraphs, a local defensive pulse, independent aim, and modern input/accessibility options. Scoring thresholds, wave timings, enemy designs, soundtrack, gibberish radio, and all procedural game art are original. Historical exact scoring tables, soundtrack, original timing values, and authentic voice cadence are **unknown**.

All audio is written in JavaScript: restrained military synthesizer motifs gain urgency across acts; separate procedural instruments distinguish laser sweeps, impact ticks, shield bursts, charge/pulse swells, tractor locks, warnings, and pickup notes. Nonverbal radio syllables pass through the engine's voice bus and accompany readable captions supplied by the simulation. No audio file, recorded voice, intelligible speech synthesis, or sound copied from the original is included. Music defaults to 10%; sound and voice have separate buses. Signal verification is reported separately from human listening.
